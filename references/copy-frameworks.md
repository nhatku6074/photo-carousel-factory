# Copy frameworks

Use a framework as a starting structure, not a mandatory script. Preserve the user's product positioning and domain constraints.

## Hook — three points — reveal

Best for habits, mistakes, myths, routines, and list-style education.

1. `3 habits making [desired outcome] harder`
2. `1. [Recognizable behavior]`
3. `2. [Less obvious behavior]`
4. `3. [Cumulative behavior or consequence]`
5. `[Simple product routine or next step]`

## Myth — truth — proof — routine — reveal

Best when the audience holds a common misconception.

1. `The [topic] advice I stopped believing`
2. `Myth: [short misconception]`
3. `What matters instead: [short correction]`
4. `The routine I could actually maintain`
5. `[Product reveal without an unsupported promise]`

## Problem — agitation — micro-action — routine — reveal

Best for a single pain point that needs emotional progression.

1. `[Specific moment] was quietly derailing me`
2. `The part nobody notices: [agitation]`
3. `Start with this: [small behavior]`
4. `Then make it repeatable: [routine]`
5. `[Product as part of the routine]`

## Writing rules

- Put one idea on each slide.
- Use concrete behaviors rather than abstract benefits.
- Keep most captions under 44 characters when possible.
- Avoid repeating the same noun at the start of every slide.
- Make the hook specific enough to pre-qualify the audience.
- Let the visuals carry context; captions should not narrate every visible detail.
- Do not invent studies, testimonials, certifications, quantities, discounts, or product claims.
- Treat regulated claims as a separate domain requirement, not a copywriting shortcut.

## Variation without drift

When creating multiple sets for the same product, change the pain-point cluster rather than merely swapping synonyms. Examples of distinct clusters include morning routine, workplace habits, shopping behavior, evening routine, travel, organization, convenience, or gifting.
